Editor Comments

"The authors estimate a Behavioral Uncovered Interest Rate Parity (BUIP) model to explain exchange rate returns across 14 emerging and advanced economies. The model departs from rational expectations by assuming market participants use two competing forecasting heuristics: a fundamentalist rule (based on Purchasing Power Parity) and a chartist (trend-following) rule. The market share of these rules evolves endogenously via a discrete-choice mechanism based on past profitability. The authors compare this structural nonlinear model against a reduced-form logistic smooth-transition regression (STR) benchmark, finding that fundamentalist forces are strongly identified, though evidence for smooth transitions (versus discontinuous jumps) is mixed.

Major Comments / Required Revisions
- Interpretation of the Switching Discontinuity: The authors note in the abstract and conclusion that "discontinuous threshold transitions [are] more frequent than smooth functions." In an evolutionary context, a smooth transition (γ<∞ in the logistic function) implies gradual social learning or heterogeneous switching costs among agents. A discontinuous jump implies herd behavior or perfectly synchronized switching. The authors should expand the theoretical discussion in Section 3 to interpret what these discontinuous jumps mean for market efficiency and systemic risk from a behavioral perspective.

- Identification Concerns in the BUIP Model: According to the appendix notes, there are potential identification concerns regarding the switching point for several countries, notably Japan, Korea, and the United Kingdom ("NO flags potential identification concerns in the switching point"). The authors must address these anomalies in the main text. Are these identification failures due to prolonged periods of zero/negative interest rate policies (ZIRP/NIRP) in these specific economies, which might break the traditional UIP mechanism?

- The "Intensity of Choice" Parameter: The discrete-choice mechanism relies on an intensity of choice parameter that dictates how fast agents switch to the more profitable rule. The authors should provide a deeper comparative analysis of this parameter across the advanced vs. emerging economies in their sample. Do emerging markets exhibit a higher intensity of choice (more aggressive switching/volatility) compared to advanced economies?

Minor Comments
- Figures 4 and 5 (Appendix): The multi-panel plots for the STR and BUIP models are highly informative, but the axes labels are quite small. Please increase the font size for readability in the final print version.

- Sample Period Dynamics: The dataset ends in 2025. It would be beneficial to briefly comment on whether the extreme volatility of the 2020-2024 period (COVID-19 pandemic, global inflation shocks, and rapid monetary tightening) disproportionately drove the identification of the fundamentalist regimes compared to the pre-2020 low-volatility period.

- Literature Update: Ensure that recent empirical tests of behavioral macro-finance models (2022-2025) published in the Journal of Evolutionary Economics and Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control are adequately cited in the introduction to properly situate the paper at the current frontier of the field.
