Referee Report : Nonlinear Exchange Rate Dynamics and Behavioral Expectations: 

New Evidence from Selected Emerging and Advanced Economies 

February 9, 2026 

Journal: Journal of Evolutionary Economics 

# Short Summary

The paper estimates a behavioral uncovered interest parity (BUIP) model where exchange-rate returns are determined by interest-rate differential and market expectations formed by heterogeneous à la Brock and Hommes (1997) . One class of agents, the fundamentalists, anchors expectations to a long run PPP fundamental. The other class, the chartists, adopt a trend-following rule. The share of fundamentalist and chartist agents evolve endogenously by a discrete-choice model driven by a performance measure. As a non-linear benchmark, also a reduced-form smooth transition regression (STR) is estimated. The two models are employed on an unbalanced panel of 14 currencies ; estimates are compared and differences across countries investigated are analyzed. 

# General Assessment

This paper addresses a relevant question in international finance, namely the ability of heterogeneous agent behavioural models to explain the well-documented puzzles in exchange rates across different countries. The research is theoretically grounded, describing a clear and well defined framework. The empirical investigation is also methodologically sound. 

However, to fully realize this potential, the authors should enhance the interpretation and synthesis of their heterogeneous and sometimes puzzling results. The current discussion often stops at stating mixed or market-dependent outcomes, without probing into the economic or institutional factors that could explain this variation. 

# Major Comments

1. One of the core findings of the paper is the pronounced heterogeneity across financial markets. While this is interesting, the reader is often left wondering on what drives this heterogeneity. For example, Table 7 shows that neither the structural BUIP nor the reduced-form STR is a uniformly superior model. The BUIP is preferred by AIC in four cases (Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, Turkey), the STR in three (Japan, Korea, Philippines), and the linear model in the rest. Furthermore, the instances of "poor identification" (threshold/cost parameter outside the data range) are almost entirely non-overlapping between the two nonlinear models (except for the UK). This result is somewhat puzzling and requires further discussion. 

The heterogeneity could be related to the nature of the selected fundamental ; for countries where PPP represents a poor, uninformative anchor (perhaps because of a history of price instability), the BUIP forcing PPP-based fundamentalist rule does not perform as well as a reduced-form model that can latch onto a different transition variable. Conversely, where PPP is empirically relevant, the 

additional structure provided by the BUIP can be an asset. This working hypothesis is suggested here and there throughout the text, but insufficiently developed. A discussion linking model performance to country-specific characteristic would greatly benefit the paper by adding some economic rationale to the findings. 

2. Relatedly, the authors have ventilated, as an avenue of future research, the possibility to adopt alternative fundamental anchors in the BUIP model. Given the mixed evidence on PPP relevance, it would be appropriate to run this robustness check within the current analysis, as it could help shed light on the underlying drivers of heterogeneity. As an alternative, a structured discussion of potential candidates as informative anchors would be helpful. 

# Minor Comments

Tables should be self-contained and readable without reference to other parts of the paper. For example, in Table 2, the notation should be recalled in the caption so as not to require the reader to move back and forth within the manuscript. 

Tables 3 & 5: The confidence intervals for some parameters (e.g., γ, c) are extremely wide, spanning orders of magnitude. This visually underscores the identification issues. The authors might consider a note in the caption or text explicitly acknowledging that these large intervals are indicative of the difficulty in precisely estimating the smoothness parameter in STR models. 

Visualisation: in all Figures, axis labels, legends and titles are very small and quite hard to read. 

Typos and inconsistencies: I recommend extensive and careful proofreading to correct typos and inconsistencies in the draft. Some examples are: 

- ‘Camberra’ should be ‘Canberra’ in the title page affiliation; 

- ‘interest-rate differential’ and ‘interest rate differential’ (or interest rate parity) are used inconsistenlty throughout the paper, stick to one (maybe without thr hyphen) 

- In footnote 1 (page 10), a reference is probably missing (Proaño (2011,?)) and the realized profit component is ill-formatted 

-‘emergent’ and ‘emerging’ countries are used inconsistently throughout the paper. The standard should be ‘emerging’ 

-There is a mix of ‘U.S.’ (with periods) and ‘US’ without. The text should be made consistent.
