# Report for “Nonlinear Exchange Rate Dynamics and Behavioral Expectations: New Evidence from Selected Emerging and Advanced Economies”

This paper aims to estimate econometrically a simple heterogeneous agents model with chartists and fundamentalists, in order to test whether profitability-based switching rules can help explain real-time exchange rate dynamics, using a large set of both developed and developing countries data. 

Overall, I consider that the paper is interesting and well constructed. I do not have specific remarks concerning the econometric strategy, I only have a couple of general remarks: 

1) The authors might consider reducing section 2 (the literature review is quite long and not always necessary for the purpose of the paper: why discussing monetary, overshooting and portfolio balance models?) 

2) The authors might further discuss the empirical validity of the assumption at the core of the model, namely that agents switch strategy according to the relative profitability of each strategy, computed in the same time horizon, even though such an assumption is highly adopted in the literature. The authors acknowledge, in the literature review, that chartists and fundamentalists trade on different time horizons: fundamentalists trade on long horizons, chartists trade on short horizons. Therefore, it is not easy to understand why traders should converge towards a common period (1 period, in the model) to evaluate ex-post their strategy and decide whether switching or not. In other words, if I am a fundamentalist trader and decide to buy 1 dollar to sell it back in 1 month, I will not observe daily fluctuations to evaluate my strategy. Conversely, if I am a chartist trader and I buy 1 dollar to sell it back within one day, I will not wait one month to evaluate my strategy. Therefore, why a one-period depreciation of the dollar should push a fundamentalist trader - who bought 1 dollar to sell it back after 1 month - to change strategy and become chartist? Statistical surveys among traders reveal that chartist traders trade on very short horizons, up to few weeks, fundamentalist traders trade on longer term horizons, up to months. Therefore, there cannot be a common time horizon for chartists and fundamentalists to make valuable comparisons of their strategies. Moreover, fundamentalist traders need fundamental models, which require a cost (the parameter k in authors’ model) but also an economic education to evaluate the good fundamentals and time to elaborate a good model. Why should a fundamentalist trader that invested in gathering information and building a fundamental model should suddenly become chartist when short-term fluctuations go to the 

opposite direction compared to their long-term forecasts, and potentially become fundamentalist again after few periods if short term fluctuations become consistent with long term forecasting? Statistical surveys show that fundamentalist traders are mostly investment funds managers, while chartist traders are mostly private traders, suggesting that the choice of the strategy is a structural feature rather than a cyclical one. Although I understand that profitability-based switching rules are widely adopted and allow producing artificial fluctuations consistent with empirical fluctuations, there appear a strong inconsistency between such an assumption and surveys among real traders. The authors might thus further discuss this assumption to reconcile it with statistical surveys’ results, maybe referring to Bassi et al (2023), a paper published in this Journal that explicitly addresses this discussion.
